How to Negotiate the Best Home Price: Strategies That Actually Work

By Agent Asad Realtor  |  September 2026  |  8 min read

Back to Blog

Negotiation is the single most important skill in real estate — and the one most buyers and sellers leave entirely to chance. Agent Asad holds a Mastery in Negotiations certification from eCornell. Here he shares the principles and tactics that consistently win at the table — whether you're buying or selling.

The Foundation: Know Your Numbers Cold

Every successful negotiation starts with data, not emotion. Before making or countering any offer, you need:

  • Comparable sales (comps): What similar homes sold for in the last 90 days within 0.5 miles
  • Days on market: How long has the home been listed? Longer = more seller motivation
  • List-to-sale ratio: Are homes in this neighborhood selling above or below asking?
  • Price reduction history: Has this home been reduced? How many times? By how much?
  • Seller's situation: Are they relocating? Divorcing? Estate sale? Motivation drives flexibility.

Negotiation Tactics for Buyers

Lead with Strength, Not Price

A strong offer isn't just about price — it's about certainty. A clean pre-approved offer with minimal contingencies at 97% of asking can beat a higher offer loaded with conditions. Sellers want a deal that closes, not a high number that falls apart.

Use Inspection as Negotiation, Not Just Protection

After inspection, you have legitimate leverage. Request credits (not repairs) for issues found — cash at closing you can use however you choose. Typical inspector findings support $2,000–$10,000 in credits on an average Albany home.

The "Personal Letter" Technique

In some situations (particularly with long-term owner-occupants), a personal letter from the buyer explaining why they love the home can tip a decision. This works best when offers are close in price and the seller has emotional attachment to the property. Note: fair housing law prohibits decisions based on protected characteristics, so keep letters focused on the home, not personal details.

Escalation Clauses in Competitive Markets

An escalation clause automatically raises your offer by a set increment above the highest competing offer, up to your maximum. Include a cap you're genuinely comfortable with — and always ask to see the competing offer before escalating. In NY, sellers are not required to show you the competing offer, but it's reasonable to request.

Time the Market

Homes listed in late fall and winter attract fewer buyers — which means less competition and more negotiating power. If you can be flexible on timing, winter purchases often yield better prices and more concessions.

Negotiation Tactics for Sellers

Create Competitive Pressure

Set an offer deadline of 24–48 hours after listing (if your market is active). Communicate that you're expecting multiple offers. This creates urgency that drives buyers toward their best and final number earlier.

Counter on Terms, Not Just Price

Sometimes a lower offer with a faster closing, fewer contingencies, and a larger deposit is worth more than a higher offer with 30-day contingencies and a shaky mortgage. Price is one variable. Evaluate the whole package.

Use Multiple Offers to Your Advantage — Ethically

When you have multiple offers, you can ask all parties to submit their "highest and best." In New York, you're not required to disclose other offers' exact prices, but honesty is always the best policy — it maintains trust through closing.

Don't Respond to Lowball Offers Emotionally

A lowball offer is information: this buyer is interested enough to engage. Counter within 5% of your asking price. A counter keeps the negotiation alive; silence or rejection ends it. A deal that starts at 85% of asking often closes at 96%.

Asad's Negotiation Principle: The best deals aren't won by being aggressive — they're won by understanding what the other party needs and finding creative ways to give it to them while protecting your own position. A deal that works for both sides closes. A deal where one side "wins" often falls apart before closing.
