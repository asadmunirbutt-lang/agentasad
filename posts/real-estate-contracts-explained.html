Real Estate Contracts Explained: What Every Buyer and Seller Must Know

By Agent Asad Realtor  |  September 2026  |  8 min read

Back to Blog

The purchase contract is the most important document in any real estate transaction. Yet most buyers and sellers sign it without fully understanding what they've agreed to. This guide breaks down every major element of a New York residential real estate contract in plain English — so you know exactly what you're signing.

How New York Contracts Work

In New York, the real estate purchase process has two stages most states don't have:

  • The accepted offer — verbal or written, but NOT legally binding. Either party can walk away at this stage.
  • The signed purchase contract — after attorney review and approval by both sides, this creates a legally binding agreement.

This attorney review period — typically 3–7 business days — is unique to New York and gives both parties critical protection. Use it fully.

Key Elements of Every Purchase Contract

Purchase Price

The agreed price. May include an escalation clause if you're in a multiple-offer situation — this automatically raises your offer by a set increment above competing bids, up to a maximum.

Earnest Money (Contract Deposit)

In New York, the buyer typically puts down 10% of the purchase price as a contract deposit at signing (not at offer acceptance). This money is held in escrow and applied to your down payment at closing. If you default without a valid contingency, you may forfeit this deposit.

Closing Date

The target date for transferring ownership. In New York, closings typically occur 60–90 days after contract signing. The date is usually "on or about" — meaning it can shift by a reasonable amount without breach.

Included Property (Fixtures vs. Personal Property)

The contract specifies what stays with the home (fixtures — permanently attached items) and what the seller can take (personal property). Kitchen appliances, washer/dryer, light fixtures, and window treatments are common negotiation points. Get every item you expect to receive in writing.

The Most Important Contingencies

Inspection Contingency

Gives you the right to inspect the property and negotiate repairs, credits, or walk away if serious defects are found. Usually 10–15 days from contract signing. Never waive this in New York unless you truly understand the property's condition.

Mortgage Contingency

Protects you if your loan application is denied. If you cannot secure financing by a specified date, you can cancel the contract and get your deposit back. Typically 30–45 days. Critical protection for any financed purchase.

Appraisal Contingency

If the home appraises below the purchase price, you can renegotiate or walk away. Important in a competitive market where buyers sometimes offer above asking price — if the bank's appraisal comes in low, this contingency protects you from being forced to make up the difference in cash.

Sale Contingency

Allows you to cancel the purchase if your current home doesn't sell first. Less common in seller's markets — many sellers won't accept this contingency — but available when negotiating power shifts to buyers.

Default: What Happens If One Party Backs Out?

Buyer default: If a buyer backs out after the attorney review period without a valid contingency, the seller typically keeps the contract deposit (10% of purchase price). The seller may also sue for specific performance — forcing the buyer to complete the purchase — though this is rare.

Seller default: If a seller backs out after contract signing, the buyer can sue for specific performance (forcing the sale) or recover their deposit plus damages. Courts take seller defaults seriously in New York.

Read the Rider: New York contracts often include a "rider" — additional clauses added by the parties' attorneys. These riders contain some of the most important terms: specific inspection rights, what happens if the property is damaged before closing, lead paint disclosures, and more. Read your rider carefully.

The Closing Statement

At closing, both parties receive a final settlement statement showing every dollar that changes hands. Review this with your attorney before signing. Common items:

  • Purchase price and any credits
  • Prorated property taxes and HOA dues
  • Lender fees (for buyers)
  • Attorney fees, title insurance, recording fees
  • NY transfer tax (paid by seller: 0.4% of sale price)
  • Mansion tax (paid by buyer: 1% on purchases over $1 million)
