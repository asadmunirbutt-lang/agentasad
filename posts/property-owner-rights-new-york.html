Property Owner Rights in New York: What Every Homeowner Should Know

By Agent Asad Realtor  |  September 2026  |  8 min read

Back to Blog

Owning property in New York comes with a robust set of legal rights — and some important limitations. Whether you're dealing with noisy neighbors, government takings, zoning boards, or property tax disputes, knowing your rights puts you in control. Here's what every New York homeowner should understand.

The Right to Use and Enjoy Your Property

New York common law protects your right to "quiet enjoyment" of your property — the right to use it without unreasonable interference from neighbors or government. This includes the right to:

  • Make improvements and renovations (subject to local building codes and permits)
  • Plant gardens, build fences, and landscape within setback and zoning rules
  • Operate a home-based business (subject to local zoning)
  • Rent out your home or a portion of it (subject to local regulations)

Your Right to Protest Property Tax Assessments

In New York, property owners have the right to contest their assessed property value if they believe it is unfair. The grievance process is straightforward:

  • File a grievance with your local Board of Assessment Review during the annual grievance period (typically May in most NY municipalities)
  • If denied, appeal to the Small Claims Assessment Review (SCAR) court — no attorney required for owner-occupied residential properties
  • Bring comparable sales data (comps) to support your case
STAR Exemption Reminder: New York's School Tax Relief (STAR) program reduces school taxes for eligible homeowners. Apply through your local assessor's office if you haven't already.

Your Rights Against Eminent Domain

The government can take private property for public use under eminent domain — but it must pay "just compensation." In New York, you have the right to:

  • Receive written notice of any proposed taking
  • Hire an independent appraiser to contest the offered compensation
  • Negotiate the amount in an Eminent Domain Procedure Law (EDPL) proceeding
  • Appeal a final determination to the Appellate Division

Your Rights in Zoning and Land Use Disputes

Local zoning laws govern how you can use your property. If a zoning decision negatively affects your property — or if a neighbor's project threatens your quiet enjoyment — you have rights:

  • Attend and speak at local planning and zoning board meetings
  • Apply for a variance to deviate from zoning requirements
  • Appeal zoning board decisions to the Supreme Court via an Article 78 proceeding
  • Challenge neighbor violations with your local code enforcement office

Your Right to Privacy — Protecting Against Trespass

New York law strongly protects homeowners from trespass. No one — including government inspectors in most circumstances — can enter your property without permission or a court-issued warrant. Post "No Trespassing" signs to strengthen your legal position. If utility companies have easements on your property, those easement terms define their access rights — not a blanket right to enter whenever they choose.

HOA Rights

If your property is in a Homeowners Association (HOA), you have the right to attend meetings, vote on rules, inspect financial records, and challenge rule changes that weren't properly adopted. HOAs in New York must follow their own bylaws and the Not-for-Profit Corporation Law.

Your Rights as a Landlord (If You Rent)

If you rent part or all of your property, New York law creates specific obligations — and rights — on both sides. As an owner-landlord, you have the right to:

  • Collect a security deposit (up to one month's rent under recent NYS law)
  • Pursue eviction through Housing Court for non-payment or lease violations
  • Access the property for repairs with proper advance notice (usually 24 hours)
  • Increase rent (subject to local rent stabilization laws in some municipalities)

Your Right to Sell, Transfer, or Mortgage Your Property

As a property owner, you have broad rights to transfer ownership — by sale, gift, inheritance, or trust. No government entity can prevent you from selling your home to a qualified buyer. However, if your property has liens (unpaid taxes, contractor liens, mortgage balances), those must be resolved at closing before clear title can transfer.
